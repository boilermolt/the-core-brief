### 1. NRC Proposes Major Inspection Overhaul
**Source:** [Nuclear Newswire (ANS)](https://www.ans.org/news/2026-02-26/article-7801/nrc-staff-proposes-rop-security-inspection-overhauls/)

The NRC staff has proposed the most significant changes to reactor oversight and security inspection in more than two decades. Two staff papers—SECY-26-0014 and SECY-26-0015—released February 18th outline sweeping revisions to the Reactor Oversight Process (ROP) and baseline security inspection programs.

**The ROP changes** would cut annualized inspection hours from 2,018 to 1,245—a nearly 40% reduction. Staff propose eliminating certain baseline inspection procedures entirely while transferring their risk-significant elements into other existing procedures. The changes also revise the "more-than-minor" (MTM) screening criteria used to determine whether issues warrant enforcement action, aiming to "reduce subjectivity, improve consistency, and better align enforcement actions with their actual impact to public health and safety."

**Security inspection** would see eight of eleven baseline inspection procedures retired and consolidated into two new procedures: "Security Operations" and "Security Performance." The material control and accounting inspection would shift from triennial to "as-needed" based on licensee performance, while cybersecurity inspections would move from biennial to triennial frequency.

**Force-on-Force (FOF) exercises**—which simulate security threats at facilities—would be restructured from multiple inspections to a single triennial inspection with two exercises. Perhaps most notably, the NRC proposes eliminating the "effective/ineffective/indeterminate" outcome labels in favor of a continuous-improvement model. Licensees would also gain the option to develop their own exercise scenarios (subject to NRC approval), giving operators more ownership of their security readiness testing.

**Why it matters:** These changes stem from both the 2024 ADVANCE Act—which added "efficiency" to the NRC's mission—and President Trump's executive order requiring an NRC review process overhaul. For plant staff, this represents a fundamental shift in how regulatory performance is measured and how inspection resources are allocated. The reduced inspection hours don't mean less oversight—they reflect a move toward more risk-informed, performance-based regulation. But the devil is in the implementation. Watch for the comment period; if you're in RP, ops, or security, this will directly affect your day-to-day compliance priorities.

**Keywords:** `inspection` `reactor` `NRC` `ROP` `security`

### 2. IAEA Study Reveals Radiation Dose Variation in Cardiac Imaging
**Source:** [IAEA Press Release](http://www.iaea.org/newscenter/pressreleases/iaea-coordinated-research-finds-variation-in-radiation-doses-from-cardiac-imaging-highlights-areas-to-enhance-patient-safety) | [JAMA Publication](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/10.1001/jama.2026.0703)

A major international study published in JAMA has revealed significant global variation in radiation doses delivered during diagnostic imaging for coronary artery disease (CAD). The research—coordinated by the IAEA and Columbia University—analyzed data from over 19,000 patients across 742 centers in 101 countries, making it the largest global assessment of radiation exposure from non-invasive cardiac imaging.

**The findings are stark:** patients in low- and middle-income countries often received significantly higher radiation doses for the same tests, particularly for coronary CT angiography. The study found that "where a patient lives, the resources available to a health system and how those resources are used can strongly influence how much radiation they receive—even when undergoing the same test," according to principal investigator Dr. Andrew Einstein, Director of Nuclear Cardiology at Columbia University.

The variation wasn't random. Centers with optimized protocols and newer technology consistently delivered lower doses while maintaining or improving diagnostic quality. The authors emphasize that dose reduction doesn't mean compromising test results—in many cases, the technology and knowledge to reduce exposure already exist. The challenge is ensuring consistent, equitable application worldwide.

**Why it matters:** This is medical radiation safety at scale, and the lessons extend beyond cardiology. CAD remains the leading cause of death globally, and cardiac imaging use has climbed steadily for decades. The study demonstrates that dose optimization isn't just about equipment—it's about training, standardized protocols, and quality assurance systems. For those of us in radiation protection, the parallels to industrial/power reactor ALARA programs are obvious: you can have the best technology in the world, but without procedural discipline and institutional commitment, doses drift upward. The IAEA plans to use these findings to inform future training programs and develop regional dose reference levels—exactly the kind of data-driven approach that works in our industry too.

**Keywords:** `IAEA` `radiation` `dose` `ALARA` `medical imaging`

### 3. Diablo Canyon Clears License Renewal Hurdle
**Source:** [Reddit: r/NuclearPower](https://reddit.com/r/NuclearPower/comments/1rgww63/diablo_canyon_has_cleared_one_of_the_last_hurdles/)

California's last operating nuclear plant has cleared one of the final regulatory hurdles for license renewal, marking a dramatic reversal from the state's 2016 commitment to shut down the plant by 2025. Diablo Canyon's two units—totaling 2,200 MWe—now appear on track for extended operation into the 2030s.

The plant's path has been anything but linear. Originally slated for closure, a combination of energy reliability concerns, climate goals, and political pressure led Governor Gavin Newsom to champion a $1.4 billion state bailout in 2022 to keep the lights on. Federal support followed, including a $1.1 billion conditional loan from the Department of Energy. Now, with regulatory clearance advancing, the question shifts from "will it survive?" to "what does the license extension process actually look like?"

**Why it matters:** Diablo Canyon is a bellwether for nuclear's political and technical viability in traditionally hostile territory. If California—ground zero for anti-nuclear activism in the 1970s—can extend a plant's license, it signals a broader shift in how states are weighing reliability and carbon emissions against decades-old phase-out commitments. Technically, the license renewal process for aging plants is well-trodden, but each application reveals new insights into long-term component degradation, maintenance strategies, and regulatory expectations for extended operation. Expect other operators with units approaching their initial license expiration to watch closely. This isn't just a California story—it's a template.

**Keywords:** `license renewal` `NRC` `Diablo Canyon` `extended operation`

### 4. Kairos Power Validates Shielding Design Through Full-Scale Testing
**Source:** [Nuclear Newswire (ANS)](https://www.ans.org/news/2026-02-27/article-7804/tests-back-shielding-plan-for-kairos-power/)

Kairos Power has completed full-scale radiation shielding tests on a precast concrete structure at its Oak Ridge Reactor Demonstration Campus, using an iridium-192 source to measure radiation transmission through walls, joints, and seams. The tests validate the company's plan to use modular precast concrete construction—the same technique used for tunnels and bridges—for its fluoride salt-cooled high-temperature reactor (FHR) designs.

**The testing setup:** Kairos built a 26-foot-tall shielding structure from precast concrete elements, connected with various joint designs (some grouted, others not). An industrial Ir-192 source—commonly used in radiography and medical treatment—was directed at the structure to measure whether radiation penetrated gaps, seams, or imperfections. Results closely matched engineering model predictions, and critically, certain joint configurations performed well without grouting—a potential schedule and cost win.

"One of the major drivers of schedule for precast concrete is grout, which can ultimately increase the cost for the project," explained Kairos principal engineer John Perry. "If we can evaluate that certain joints don't need to be grouted or are successful from a radiation perspective and a shielding perspective, that's a boon for our company."

**What's next:** The data will directly inform the design and construction of the Hermes low-power demonstration reactor, currently under construction at Oak Ridge. Hermes—a 35 MWt FHR using TRISO pebble fuel and FLiBe molten salt coolant—was the first non-light water reactor to receive an NRC construction permit (December 2023). It's the first step in Kairos's "iterative pathway" toward a 150 MWe commercial KP-FHR design.

**Why it matters:** Advanced reactor designs use fundamentally different coolants, fuels, and operating conditions than traditional LWRs, which means radiation shielding strategies can't simply be copied from existing plants. Demonstrating that modular construction techniques can meet regulatory dose limits is a critical licensing and economics milestone. Precast concrete is faster to install and potentially cheaper than poured-in-place methods, but only if it delivers equivalent shielding performance. Kairos just proved it can. For RP professionals, this is a preview of the kind of first-principles shielding work that will define Gen IV reactor licensing.

**Keywords:** `reactor` `radiation` `shielding` `advanced reactors` `Kairos` `TRISO`

### 5. NRC Ends Work on Spent Fuel Security Rules
**Source:** [Nuclear Newswire (ANS)](https://www.ans.org/news/2026-02-26/article-7800/nrc-ends-work-on-three-proposed-rules-for-securing-spent-fuel/)

The NRC has formally ended development of three proposed rules related to securing spent nuclear fuel, closing the books on regulatory initiatives that had been in various stages of development. The decision reflects shifting regulatory priorities and constrained agency bandwidth in the wake of the ADVANCE Act and recent executive orders.

The shelved rules addressed spent fuel security requirements, but specifics on their scope and the NRC's rationale for discontinuation remain limited in public documents. The move comes as the NRC faces pressure to streamline operations, reduce regulatory burden, and focus resources on licensing new reactor designs and advanced fuels.

**Why it matters:** Spent fuel security and long-term storage remain politically and technically contentious. The U.S. still lacks a permanent geological repository, and tens of thousands of metric tons of spent fuel sit in dry cask storage at plant sites nationwide. Security requirements for this material—particularly at decommissioned sites—have been debated for years, with industry pushing back against what they see as overly prescriptive rules that don't reflect actual threat assessments.

The NRC's decision to shelve these rules may signal reduced concern about certain threat scenarios, or it may simply reflect resource allocation trade-offs as the agency pivots toward advanced reactor licensing. Either way, the lack of movement on spent fuel policy continues a decades-long pattern of regulatory and political gridlock. For operators managing on-site storage, the status quo persists—but don't mistake inaction for resolution.

**Keywords:** `NRC` `spent fuel` `security` `repository` `dry cask storage`

## What to Watch

- **NRC inspection overhaul comment period:** Should open within weeks. If you work in plant operations, radiation protection, or security, read SECY-26-0014 and SECY-26-0015 and submit comments. These changes will affect how your performance is measured.

- **Diablo Canyon final EIS:** Expected in the coming months. The license extension process will set precedent for other aging plants seeking to extend operation beyond their initial 40- or 60-year licenses.

- **Advanced reactor shielding data:** As more Gen IV designs (FHRs, MSRs, SFRs) move toward construction permits, expect increasing public disclosure of radiation protection strategies, source term analyses, and shielding validation studies. Kairos's precast concrete testing is just the beginning.

- **Spent fuel policy (or lack thereof):** With the NRC shelving new security rules and Yucca Mountain still in political purgatory, on-site dry cask storage remains the long-term reality for most plants. Keep an eye on state-level legislation and any movement on consolidated interim storage proposals.
